\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

\section{Background of Low-Cost Mobile Platforms}
\label{sec:background}

An autonomous mobile platform must repeatedly convert sensor measurements into
motion commands while operating within the limits of its sensing, computation
and actuation hardware. For a small vehicle, the practical problem includes
obtaining usable range measurements, representing the surrounding obstacles,
selecting a steering action and applying that action before the observation
becomes outdated. These stages form a coupled measurement and control system.

Low-cost ARM microcontrollers provide a practical basis for building and
studying such systems. Their communication peripherals and motor-control
interfaces allow sensing and actuation to be integrated on a compact platform.
The Teensy 4.0 board selected for this project uses an ARM Cortex-M7 processor
and provides serial and pulse-width modulation interfaces suitable for the
vehicle hardware~\cite{pjrcTeensy40}. However, its finite program memory, working
memory and available processing time impose limits on the size and execution
cost of a learned controller. Model selection must account for these limits
alongside prediction performance.

Light detection and ranging (LiDAR) supplies distance observations that can be
used to select steering actions in a local environment. In this project, planar
range measurements are converted into a fixed-length input representation for
the control model. The representation must preserve a consistent relationship
between angular position and distance across data collection, simulation and
embedded inference. Missing returns, angular coverage and observation age also
affect the information available to the controller. These properties make the
sensor interface an essential part of the learning problem.

The task considered in this dissertation is continuous local obstacle avoidance
and cruising at a fixed target forward speed. A differential-drive vehicle
selects discrete steering actions from LiDAR observations. Autonomous navigation
is used in this specific sense throughout the study.

\section{Motivation and Problem Statement}
\label{sec:motivation}

Training a supervised controller requires observations paired with suitable
actions. Collecting these examples entirely through physical driving requires
repeated operation of the vehicle and consistent action labels. Simulation
provides a way to generate demonstrations while controlling the map, initial
pose and vehicle parameters. It also makes geometric and physical state
information available that would require additional sensing or estimation on
the real platform.

The proposed approach uses this additional information to train a teacher
policy. Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) alternates between collecting
experience and updating a policy through a surrogate objective
function~\cite{schulman2017ppo}. In the present implementation, the teacher
observes a local distance field derived from the simulated map together with
selected vehicle-state variables. During demonstration collection, simulated
LiDAR scans are recorded alongside the teacher's executed actions. This produces
a supervised dataset for a student controller that receives only LiDAR input.

A random forest is selected as the student model. Random forests combine
multiple decision trees to produce a prediction~\cite{breiman2001random}.
Restricting tree depth and tree count gives this project an explicit means of
controlling the number of decisions performed during inference. The trained
forest can also be exported as C++ inference code. Whether a particular forest
meets the target board's resource and timing requirements must still be
established by measurement.

The teacher and student do not receive equivalent information. Vehicle state
and map-derived geometry available to the teacher may not be recoverable from
a single student scan. Consequently, disagreement with a teacher action cannot
automatically be attributed to insufficient student model capacity. This
information difference must be considered when interpreting the achievable
action-prediction performance.

Closed-loop operation introduces a further difficulty: an action changes the
vehicle's subsequent observations. A student can therefore encounter states
that differ from those represented in its demonstrations. This departure from
independent supervised prediction is a central issue in imitation
learning~\cite{ross2011reduction}. Offline action accuracy is consequently
insufficient as the sole measure of controller quality. A small steering error
may be corrected on a later step or may accumulate into an unsafe trajectory.

The central problem addressed by this dissertation is how to construct and
evaluate a LiDAR-based student controller from privileged simulation
demonstrations while retaining a credible path to a low-cost ARM implementation.
This requires consistent sensor and action definitions, traceable training data,
an appropriately constrained model and separate evidence for prediction,
closed-loop driving and embedded execution.

\section{Research Objectives and Questions}
\label{sec:objectives}

The overall objective is to develop and evaluate a pipeline that connects
privileged teacher demonstrations with a lightweight LiDAR-based controller
intended for an ARM microcontroller. The specific objectives are:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Establish a vehicle and simulation interface with explicit LiDAR
  representation, discrete action definitions and control timing.
  \item Generate traceable LiDAR--action demonstrations using a PPO teacher,
  including perturbed initial poses, near-wall starts and controlled input
  dropout augmentation.
  \item Train a constrained random forest and evaluate action prediction using
  a map-based separation between fitting and validation data.
  \item Assess the student in closed-loop simulation using completion, failure,
  progress and clearance measurements, with sensor-loss conditions evaluated
  separately from normal operation.
  \item Export the learned model to C++, verify inference consistency and
  establish the integration and measurement requirements for target-board and
  physical-vehicle evaluation.
\end{enumerate}

These objectives lead to three research questions:

\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1:] \textbf{Learning from demonstrations.}
  Can a random forest trained on privileged-teacher demonstrations reproduce
  useful steering behaviour from LiDAR alone on a map excluded from forest
  fitting?
  \item[RQ2:] \textbf{Closed-loop behaviour and robustness.}
  How does offline action-prediction performance relate to closed-loop driving,
  and how is that driving affected by missing range observations and changes in
  the initial state or map?
  \item[RQ3:] \textbf{Embedded feasibility.}
  What model resources, execution times and interface conditions determine
  whether the controller can be used on the target ARM vehicle, and what
  additional evidence is needed to establish physical driving performance?
\end{description}

The questions require different forms of evidence. Held-out prediction metrics
address action imitation, repeated driving episodes address interaction with
the environment, and target-board measurements address embedded feasibility.
Success at one level does not by itself establish success at the others.

\section{Scope and Constraints}
\label{sec:scope}

The experimental platform combines a Teensy 4.0 vehicle implementation with a
ROS 2 and Gazebo Classic simulation. The current simulated driving task uses
static obstacles, a target forward speed of approximately
\(0.2\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), and fifteen steering labels. Both wheel targets
remain positive within the steering action set. The investigated behaviour is
therefore forward cruising and local avoidance within roads compatible with
the vehicle's turning capability. Goal-directed route planning, simultaneous
localisation and mapping, dynamic-obstacle interaction, reversing and in-place
rotation are outside the current task definition.

The student input contains 200 distance columns expressed in millimetres. Only
the configured front angular sector carries range observations; columns outside
that sector remain zero. Missing measurements can also appear as zeros within
the active sector. An input width of 200 must therefore be distinguished from
200 independently observed directions. The current baseline retains all columns
and uses a forest with 88 trees and a maximum depth of three.

Dataset separation is performed by map rather than by randomly distributing
adjacent frames between fitting and validation sets. Original observations and
their dropout copies retain the same map assignment. A map excluded from
student fitting is not automatically excluded from every stage of teacher
development or student model selection. These roles must be documented
separately when making claims about generalisation.

Hardware transfer remains subject to interface and measurement constraints.
The simulation steering labels and wheel-speed commands differ from the legacy
physical firmware's steering and PWM conventions. Input freshness and missing
value handling must also agree with the deployed model. Host-side C++ agreement
establishes consistency of model inference on the tested inputs; it does not
establish board-level timing, motor response or physical driving safety. Those
claims require corresponding hardware measurements and repeated vehicle tests.

\section{Contributions of the Dissertation}
\label{sec:contributions}

The contributions of the work completed at this stage concern the integration
and evaluation of established learning methods for the selected vehicle:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item A simulation data-collection pipeline connects a map-informed PPO
  teacher with a LiDAR-only student. It records observation--action pairing and
  dataset provenance, and supports initial-pose perturbations, near-wall starts
  and reproducible dropout augmentation.
  \item A random-forest training and export workflow preserves map-based
  validation, reports class-sensitive and steering-distance metrics, and
  checks the exported C++ predictions against the Python model. The current
  export report records agreement on 3,149 verification inputs.
  \item A closed-loop evaluation workflow reports driving outcomes separately
  from offline imitation metrics. In the existing baseline experiment, the
  student completed twenty 180-second episodes across four maps, travelling
  approximately 720~m with no recorded collisions. One of those maps was
  excluded from forest fitting. These observations apply to the recorded
  vehicle settings, starts and durations.
\end{enumerate}

These contributions provide an implemented basis for examining the three
research questions. They do not establish a new PPO or random-forest algorithm.
Target-board resource measurements, extended sensor-loss experiments and
quantitative physical driving results remain necessary to complete the
evaluation of embedded deployment.

\section{Organization of the Dissertation}
\label{sec:organization}

Chapter~2 reviews LiDAR-based local navigation, learning on constrained
microcontrollers, random forests, PPO, privileged teachers and simulation to
reality. Chapter~3 describes the physical platform, sensor representation,
differential-drive model and simulation interfaces. Chapter~4 presents the
teacher formulation, demonstration collection, data pairing, starting-state
perturbation and augmentation methods.

Chapter~5 describes data validation, student training, evaluation metrics, C++
export and embedded integration. Chapter~6 presents the experimental design,
available results, comparative evaluations and limitations, with offline,
closed-loop and hardware evidence distinguished throughout. Chapter~7 concludes
the dissertation by answering the research questions and identifying the
remaining work.
